## Core Belief

The foundation of good product work is customer and market insight. Everything else — strategy, prioritization, roadmap, delivery — builds on top of that. If you skip the foundation, you're guessing.

## Influences and What I Take From Them

### The Product Management Lifecycle Model
*(Product Manager's Desktop Reference)*

The lifecycle:
1. Customer & Market Insights
2. Product Strategy Formulation
3. Product Planning & Prioritization
4. Product Development & Introduction
5. Post-Launch Performance Management

**What I take from this:** The sequencing matters. You can't build a real strategy without understanding customers. You can't prioritize well without a strategy. The activities are dynamic and loop back — this is not a waterfall. The fact that customer insights anchor the whole model is the most important part.

**Where I live in this model:** My strongest experience is in steps 3 and 4 — planning, prioritization, and working with engineering through development and introduction. I've done go-to-market (at Cision this was a shared responsibility with a GTM business unit, but I was accountable for the deliverables). Post-launch performance management I've always owned — metrics, bug prioritization, iteration.

### Continuous Discovery Habits
*(Teresa Torres)*

The practice of running ongoing, lightweight customer discovery rather than big research sprints. Staying close to real users continuously so decisions are always grounded.

**What I take from this:** The opportunity solution tree is the framework I return to most for prioritization. It forces the connection between customer outcomes, the opportunities that block those outcomes, and the solutions you're considering. It prevents you from jumping to solutions before you've understood the opportunity.

**Honest gap:** I haven't been able to run full continuous discovery the way Torres describes inside an organization. The conditions at Cision — sunset platform, shrinking headcount, firefighting mode — weren't ideal for this. But it's the practice I want to build toward in my next role, and it's the framework I'd push for if I had influence over team process.

### Escaping the Build Trap
*(Melissa Perri)*

The build trap: a company mistakes output (features shipped) for progress (outcomes achieved). Product managers in a feature factory become order-takers, not strategy owners.

**What I take from this:** I want to be outcome-driven. Shipping is not the goal. The goal is the change in customer behavior or business result that the ship was supposed to create. The roadmap should be organized around outcomes, not feature lists.

## How This Maps to My Actual Career

I've been living this framework even without always naming it:

- At **Cision**, I was hired into a situation where engineering could have ignored the customer backlog entirely. My job — the real job, not just the title — was to be the voice of 3,500 enterprise accounts in a room full of engineers. I made customer signals (ticket volume, churn risk, escalation patterns) the prioritization input for the roadmap. That's applied customer insight as foundation, even in a constrained environment.

- The **opportunity tree** maps to my PI planning process: I was synthesizing inputs from CX, sales, legal, DevOps, and InfoSec into a single prioritized roadmap every quarter. That's structured opportunity mapping even if I didn't have the vocabulary for it at the time.

- **Escaping the build trap** is why my Cision wins are framed as customer outcomes (7% churn, 100% reduction in stale-data complaints) not just deliverables shipped.

## What I'm Still Building

- Deeper practice with continuous discovery in a real team environment
- More experience on the strategy formulation and customer insight side (steps 1–2 of the lifecycle model)
- Stronger muscle on the B2C side — closer proximity to end users, faster feedback loops

## Philosophy Summary (for interviews or copy)

"I think good product work starts with customers and builds up from there. Strategy without customer insight is guessing. Prioritization without strategy is noise. I want to build products where the decisions trace back to something real — a customer outcome we're trying to move, not a feature someone requested."
